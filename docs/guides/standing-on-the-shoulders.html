Standing on the Shoulders of Giants,
applied to a software factory

A teaching guide: which factory ideas transfer to a loop that builds software, which do not, and how to tell the difference.

The thesis, in five sentences

Factory ideas do not travel as techniques; they travel as concepts, and every technique carries unstated assumptions about the environment it was built for. Ask one question to find out whether those assumptions hold: is any station dedicated to one kind of work, or do all kinds pass through the same stations? Software passes every kind through the same stations, so the Toyota mechanisms apply — and since software has no standardised parts, only a standardised process, its buffers, its levelling and its learning curve all have to move from the part to the route. Four rules follow: the route never changes, output cannot be counted, capacity is a choice, and waiting is a decision. Everything else in this guide is either the arithmetic behind those four, the vocabulary for discussing them, or the practice that keeps a loop honest about whether it is following them.

How to use this guide

It is written to be read in order, and to be taught in parts. Part 1 is the source article. Parts 2 and 3 give the method that came out of it. Parts 4 to 10 are the machinery: four rules, the arithmetic behind them, the vocabulary, Kanban and just in time examined and partly refused, and mistake-proofing. Part 11 is the version people asked to run on their own loop. Part 12 is the three corrections this work made to itself and part 13 is what it declined, both kept because they teach more than the finished conclusions do. Part 14 is an honest account of what is new here and what is sixty years old. Parts 15 to 17 are for teaching: the objections you will get, three session plans, and how to run this as a question-and-answer rather than a lecture, with what a recorded course would still need.

It is a guide, not an authority. Where it disagrees with the project's own design document, or with the running code, those win — the design document is kept with the harness and is not published here, so treat this guide as the standalone version. Where it disagrees with the books, the books win and this should be corrected.

1. The source: Goldratt's Standing on the Shoulders of Giants

The article is nineteen pages, written in 2006, subtitled "Production concepts versus production applications", and its example is Hitachi Tool Engineering. Its argument is short and it is the reason this guide exists.

Ford found four concepts

Henry Ford's assembly line was the first system to make production flow. By 1926 the time from mining iron ore to a finished car, more than five thousand parts, on a train ready for delivery was 81 hours, and no car maker has matched it since. verified

Ford's line worked because it limited the floor space between two work stations. When the space was full, the worker feeding it had to stop. That is the whole of it: the limit on space is what stopped overproduction, and it did so without asking anyone to be disciplined. The four concepts underneath:

  1. Improving flow, or equivalently lead time, is a primary objective.
  2. That objective must be translated into a practical mechanism that says when not to produce. Ford used space.
  3. Local efficiencies must be abolished. A station that runs flat out ahead of a slow one builds a queue, and the queue hides the problem and lengthens the lead time.
  4. A focusing process must balance the flow. Ford used direct observation: a stopped line is visible, so the cause was easy to find.

Note what concept 3 costs. Acknowledging it meant telling workers to stop producing while the next station was busy, which sounds like telling them they are not needed. The article is explicit that this is the most resisted part of any such system, and that it was resisted at Toyota for over a decade.

Ohno kept the concepts and built different machinery

Toyota could not copy Ford's line. Ford's line pays only when each station is dedicated to one part, which needs volume per part. Toyota made small numbers of many different cars, so the same machines had to serve every kind. Everyone at the time concluded that flow lines were impossible in that situation. Taiichi Ohno concluded something narrower: the concepts were general, and only the machinery was tied to high volume. verified

His problem was concrete. If you limit a single space between two stations and the stations produce many kinds of parts, the space fills with parts the assembly line cannot use yet, and the line gridlocks. His answer came from American supermarkets: a shelf holds a small amount of each product, and only when a customer takes one does someone refill that product's space. That gave him a limit per part instead of a limit per space, and the mechanism for it is a card.

The card does two jobs, and both matter later in this guide. It authorizes the upstream station to make one more container, and because it names the part, it tells that station what to prepare for. Permission and forecast in one artifact.

Two years of facts worth keeping: Toyota published everything, invited competitors into its plants, and still fewer than 20% of Japanese manufacturers implemented Lean, and many that tried failed. verified Knowledge was not the constraint.

The assumption nobody had stated: stability

Goldratt's explanation of the failures is the part to teach. Toyota's machinery assumes a stable environment in three ways:

  • The products and the processes do not change much for a long time. A car model changes once a year and most parts carry over. Toyota's supplier-support organisation reports that a Lean implementation takes a minimum of six to nine months per production line, so the machinery needs months of a stable product to pay for itself. verified
  • Demand for each individual product is steady, because the mechanism holds a buffer per product permanently. One order per quarter does not fit.
  • The total load on each kind of resource is steady. If the load swings, a mechanism that prevents producing ahead of time causes missed dates in the busy week. Toyota restricts the mix change from one month to the next to keep this true, a condition most companies cannot impose on their customers.

Hitachi Tool Engineering had none of the three: 20,000 products, a new product family every six months, demand for most products arriving in ones and twos, and swinging load. Hitachi tried Lean repeatedly and went back each time. The article's conclusion, which is the whole method: an application makes assumptions about its environment, usually unstated; state them, and you can tell which parts apply and which you must redesign.

What Hitachi did instead

Keep the four concepts; change the base of the "stop producing" mechanism from inventory to time. Do not release the material for an order until a set interval before its due date. Add a coarse priority rule — three colours from how much of that interval has passed, ties broken arbitrarily — and reject the finer calculation, because in a noisy environment a calculation more precise than the noise makes the result worse.

Results, from the article: on-time delivery 40% to 85%, lead time and work in process cut in half, 20% more shipped with the same labour, distributor inventory from 8 months to 2.4, and net profit from 1.1 billion yen to 5.3 billion over five years while raw material prices rose faster than product prices. Profit margin 7.2% to 21.9%. verified

The second boundary

The article then applies its own method to its own answer. The time-based mechanism assumes the touch time is a small fraction of the lead time — about 10% in a conventional plant, under 1% once batch sizes exceed ten. In project environments touch time is most of the lead time, so the assumption fails and a different application is needed. Goldratt's own next book is that application. The chain is: concepts, an application, a boundary, another application, another boundary.

The five side lessons worth teaching

  1. Lead time is the objective and cost is the by-product. Toyota reduced setup times, defects and supplier prices without aiming at cost; aiming at cost is what produced the diminishing returns and the lip service elsewhere.
  2. Do not measure finer than the noise. Three colours beat a scheduler when the data is noisy. This is Goldratt citing Shewhart and Deming.
  3. When slack is exposed, spend it, do not remove it. The plants that cut staff after exposing slack ended worse than where they started, every time. verified
  4. A first estimate beats an optimum. Choose the time buffer at half the current lead time and move on; the next round of improvements changes the graph anyway.
  5. Running a few techniques is not implementing anything. Goldratt's own jab at companies that ran cost-saving programs and called it Lean.

2. The method: concept, application, assumption, boundary

The transferable part of the article is a procedure, not a conclusion. It has five steps, and it can be applied to any practice you are about to import, including the practices in this guide.

  1. Separate the concept from the application. "Small batches" is an application of "shorten the lead time". "Three colours of priority" is an application of "do not calculate more precisely than the data allows".
  2. Write down the environment the application assumed. Volume per part, stability of product and process, stability of demand per item, stability of load, cost of setup, what counts as a finished unit. Most of these are never stated by the people who built it.
  3. Check your own environment against that list, honestly. Two of the five facts will usually be different, and that is enough.
  4. If an assumption fails, do not force the application and do not abandon the concept. Redesign the mechanism for your environment, and expect the replacement to be simpler, as Toyota's card was simpler than Ford's floor space.
  5. Find the boundary of your own replacement. It has assumptions too. The article does this to itself in its last section, and that is the strongest thing in it.

There is a sixth step this guide adds, because a method that cannot fail is not a method: name the witness. For every mechanism you keep or build, say what observation would show it working, and what would show it broken. A mechanism with no witness is decoration.

3. Which environment you are in

The question that decides almost everything is short:

Is each station dedicated to one kind of work, or do all kinds pass through the same stations?

Dedicated stations mean high volume of one thing, which is Ford's setting: flow lines, buffers measured in floor space or container counts per part, and an economic batch quantity that usually exceeds one.

Shared stations mean many kinds in small numbers, which is Ohno's setting: the card, the pull, per-part limits, and setup time that dominates the work. Toyota's system was invented for exactly this case, which is why it has so much to say to software.

A software loop is Ohno's setting, and for two reasons that are worth saying out loud:

  • Everything goes through the same stations. In this project a document fix, a memory-safety fix and a new subsystem all pass through work, gates, review, merge and publish. No station is dedicated to a kind.
  • Setup time is comprehension. Before any station can work, something has to read the change and understand it. That is expensive, it varies with the size of the change, and it is the exact problem Ohno spent twenty years on. Every code map, onboarding document and prompt that tells an agent what the tree looks like is setup reduction.

Then one fact does not fit either setting, and it gets its own rule: in a car factory a car is a car, and in a software loop the units do not add up. A two-byte overflow fixed, plus the check that would have caught it, can be worth more than a week of document fixes, and there is no exchange rate between them.

4. The four rules

These are the four decisions that followed from the source article when it was applied to a loop that builds software. Each has a why, an example from a real run, and the failure it forbids. They are not Toyota's rules and not Goldratt's; they are the application for this environment, and the method in part 2 is what produced them.

The route never changes

The rule
Every change takes the same stations in the same order. Only the diff varies, never the route. Lean calls this standard work: the current best known sequence, written down, so improvement has a baseline.
Why
The four concepts only work on a process whose shape holds still long enough to improve. This is the one Toyota assumption a software loop does satisfy, and it is worth protecting on purpose rather than enjoying by accident.
Allows
Small batches, pull instead of push, a stated limit on work in process, reducing setup instead of enlarging the batch, lowering tolerated slack until a problem appears.
Forbids
Explaining an uneven run by calling the loop unpredictable. The loop is predictable; something violated a limit or injected a wait, and both are findable.
Example measured
Five work items in one worker turn produced an 18-to-20-minute review that consumed 70% or more of the reviewer's context. One item produced a 10-minute review at the same quality. The batch enlarged the station's setup; it did not spread it over more output. The fix was setup reduction — smaller diffs, a code map, a bootstrap document — and batching stayed correct only where the reading is genuinely shared: items on the same file, or one-line document fixes.
Consequence
The route is the factory, so editing the loop while a cycle is in flight is rebuilding the line with a car on it. Loop changes happen between cycles, with a restart afterwards. That is not a workaround; it is this rule being protected.

Output cannot be counted

The rule
No rate may become an objective. Lead time is the measure; value is a judgement made per run.
Why
Toyota could count because a car is a car. Here the units are incommensurable: no ratio converts one into another. Any "items per night" number therefore rewards the cheap item and the shallow review. That is Goodhart's law doing its ordinary work: once a number is the objective, the cheapest way to move the number becomes the job, and from inside everyone is busy and the number is rising.
Allows
Lead time as a measure, because it is observable, comparable across runs, and does not need the units to be equal. A value judgement per run, written down but deliberately not made into a metric. Lead time is a working stand-in for the cost of change, which is the quantity that actually matters and which no metric of the loop captures.
Forbids
A dashboard whose top line is a count. A number like that in a document is itself the defect, and the fix is to delete the number rather than explain it.
Example measured
An 18-minute review found two false claims that had been copied into three documents. By length it was the most expensive review of that day; by value it was the cheapest way to find those two. Every rate-based measure would have scored it worst and a three-minute skim best.

Capacity is a choice

The rule
While the loop runs below whatever is slowing it down, output equals how much work is pulled in and nothing else. So choose the pull. When a real limit appears, name it and make everything else serve it.
Why
Adding a parallel chain to an unconstrained loop moves work into a queue; it does not move more work out. Little's Law turns that into an identity rather than an intuition (part 5). And spare capacity is not waste: it is where the next increase in the pull is paid for. The plants in the article that removed such capacity ended worse than where they started.
Allows
Raising parallelism on purpose, to find the limit. A work-in-process limit with an actual number in it — the same decision an admission control system makes at the door of a network. Staggering once a station is visibly saturated.
Forbids
Tuning around a limit nobody measured. Treating headroom as waste, when the curve in part 5 says headroom near full utilisation is worth several times its arithmetic share. Any policy built on a guess about where the limit is gets tuned for a station that is not the one holding the loop back.
Example measured
A machine with 32 cores, where each chain runs a build, a unit suite, a suite that starts nested graphical test servers, and a review. Which of those saturates first was unknown. Known: the loop had never run more than a few chains at once, so the limit was far away and the pull was a preference rather than a number.

Waiting is a decision

The rule
Work waits on purpose in exactly one place: in front of whichever station is slowest, so that station never sits idle while work exists. Everywhere else, waiting is downtime somebody wrote into the loop, and each instance should become a tracked item with its cost attached. Lead time is latency; items per night is throughput. These rules set them in that order.
Why
This is Ford's and Ohno's second concept. The release rule is backpressure: the station that is behind tells the front of the loop to stop letting work in. A factory buys a queue in front of its slowest station because a starved station stops everything downstream. A queue anywhere else is inventory: it lengthens lead time, hides problems, and costs money to hold.
Allows
A deliberate queue in front of the saturated station, and filing every other stall with its duration.
Forbids
A stall with nothing working and the clock running. A fixed sleep where a completion signal exists. The same checks run three times over the same tree. A hand resolution during a merge. A branch left local while its CI is red.
Example measured
Four runs in one day ended with commits, no window and no verdict, and each needed the work re-derived. Separately, 52 commits sat local-only for a day, and behind them a CI job had been failing for months. One-second polling is not on this list: a station is working while the harness looks.

5. The arithmetic behind the rules

Three standard results carry rules that would otherwise be assertions. Each is a formula or a curve, so each can be checked against a real run.

Little's Law

work in process = throughput × lead time

It holds for any stable system, with no assumption about how work arrives or how long a single item takes. recalled Three consequences:

  • Throughput is set by the slowest station, so lead time falls only by cutting work in process or by raising that station's throughput.
  • Adding chains while the slowest station's throughput is unchanged raises work in process, and therefore raises lead time. That is an identity, not a risk to be measured.
  • Once lead time and work in process are both counted, throughput follows. If the three disagree, one of the measurements is wrong, and the disagreement shows up within a cycle. This makes the law a test of your own instrumentation rather than a theory.

A worked example, with illustrative numbers rather than measurements. Suppose a loop finishes 6 items in a working day of 8 hours, and 4 items are in flight on average. Then Little's Law says the average lead time is 4 ÷ (6/8) = 5.3 hours. If you double the chains and the slowest station's rate does not change, work in process becomes 8 and lead time becomes 10.7 hours at the same throughput. Nothing got faster. This is the arithmetic behind "capacity is a choice", and it is why an unconstrained loop that adds workers can measure a longer lead time for the same output.

The utilisation cliff

queueing delay ≈ (ρ / (1 − ρ)) × service time

This is Kingman's heavy-traffic approximation for a general queue. recalled The shape matters more than the exact constant: at 50% utilisation the wait is about one service time, at 80% about four, at 95% about twenty. A loop running near its limit therefore has a lead time that grows for no visible reason, and the last few percent of utilisation cost more delay than the first fifty. That is why headroom is a number and not a preference.

Rolled throughput yield

The fraction of items that pass every station without rework. For one review station it is the fraction approved on the first round, which makes the review-round count its inverse. recalled A cycle where every item needed a second pass has a yield problem in the process, not a set of careless workers, and the fix belongs in the process.

Why these three and not others

A borrowed term earns its place only when it brings predictive content: a formula, a threshold, or a named failure mode. A term that only renames a rule you already have is a new shorthand, and worse than plain words, because it drags in assumptions nobody examined. That test is the same one part 2 applies to machinery, turned on vocabulary.

7. Kanban: the card, the rules, the modern method

Two different things carry this name, and confusing them is the same mistake as confusing a concept with its application.

Toyota's kanban is a mechanism

A card travels with a container of parts. There are two kinds: a withdrawal card, which the next station takes when it consumes a container, and a production card, which goes back to the station that made it and authorises exactly one more container. The number of cards is fixed, and that fixed count is what bounds work in process. It went live through the Honsha plant in 1963, the whole chain of processes was joined by it around the mid-1970s, and Ohno's own account puts the total effort at about thirty years. verified

The six rules attributed to Ohno for using the card recalled:

  1. Never pass a defective product to the next process.
  2. The next process withdraws only what it needs, in the amount needed, at the time needed.
  3. Produce only what has been withdrawn.
  4. Level the production.
  5. The card is for fine-tuning, not an automatic device for minimising inventory.
  6. Stabilise and rationalise the process.

Note that two of the six are warnings against misusing the mechanism: rule 5 says the limit is not a minimisation target, which is Goodhart's law in 1948, and rule 6 says none of it works on an unstable process, which is the assumption Goldratt made explicit sixty years later.

The modern Kanban method is a different application

Built for knowledge work, named after the card rather than built from it. Its core practices verified: visualise the workflow; limit work in progress; manage flow; make process policies explicit; improve collaboratively using models and the scientific method; and — in Anderson's list — implement feedback loops. None of it needs a card, a container or a shop floor.

The founding principles are worth stating because they are a change-management position, not a production one: start with what you do now; agree to pursue incremental evolutionary change; initially respect current roles, responsibilities and titles. A team adopting this is told not to reorganise first.

What visualisation is actually for

The card authorises one more, and because it names the part it tells the upstream station what to prepare for. That second function is the one usually forgotten, and it is the one with a measurable payoff.

In this project: warming the build for an item is setup done while the station is not the constraint — external setup. But it currently runs at the start of the item's own cycle, so it sits on the route's critical time. It could run for the next item during the current item's review: the same work, moved off the critical path, with no new capacity. That is what a visible queue buys, and the log would show it, because setup is recorded as its own phase.

The requirement that makes this work at Toyota is worth copying too: changeovers must be finishable within the replenishment interval — the time between the rounds of the person who walks the route collecting finished work and returning cards. If your setup is longer than your pull interval, the mechanism cannot keep up. verified

A warning from inside Toyota

Takehiko Harada, on andon boards, hour-by-hour charts and the kanban cards: they are part of a system of sharing information, and the best use of those tools "is to foster communication and discussion between the management and the people working there. Just making things visual is not effective." verified

The rule that follows: a view nobody acts on is a dashboard, and rule 2 says what a dashboard is worth.

8. Just in time, and the part identity software does not have

There are no standardised parts, only a standardised process.

That sentence is this project's own summary of the difference, and it is worth unpacking, because it decides which half of Toyota's system transfers and which half cannot.

What just in time is, and what it needs

Just in time means making exactly what the next step needs, when it needs it, in the amount it needs. It is one of the two pillars of the Toyota system — the other is stopping the line on a defect instead of passing it on recalled — and the card described in part 7 is the device that operates it. As a sentence it is a platitude. As a mechanism it has four preconditions, and each one costs something to build:

  1. A pull signal, so nothing is made speculatively. Without it, just in time is a wish.
  2. Changeover short enough that small batches are economic. This is the precondition Ohno spent twenty years on. Toyota's die changes went from two to three hours in the 1940s to under an hour, and as low as fifteen minutes, in the 1950s, to three minutes in the 1960s. verified A small batch is only affordable once changeover is minutes.
  3. No defective parts. A defect stops the next station, so a pull system with defects does not run at low inventory; it stops. Quality is a precondition of the pull, not a benefit of it.
  4. Level demand. A pull system concentrates work in time: whatever is withdrawn now is what must be made now. If the mix arrives in lumps, the upstream stations can only respond with overtime or with stock, and stock is what just in time was meant to remove.

Why the buffer needs part numbers

The mechanism that keeps inventory low in a factory is a fixed number of containers per part number. It works because the part number recurs: the bracket made today is interchangeable with the bracket made last week, so a consumed container is refilled with an identical one, and the shelf can be sized once and then left alone.

This is the same requirement the source article states as Toyota's second stability assumption. Demand per product is steady enough that a permanent, small buffer of that product makes sense. When demand is one order per quarter, the buffer per product must be either large or absent, and neither is a pull system.

What software does not have

Two changes are never the same thing. The thousandth change is as new as the first: different content, different files, different failure modes. Three consequences follow.

  • There is nothing to refill. A container holding one change is the change itself. It is the finished product, not a buffer for it.
  • Finished work cannot be stocked. An unmerged change decays as its branch drifts from the trunk, and an unreviewed change has negative value, because it must be re-derived or thrown away. A factory can build ahead and hold the parts; a software loop that builds ahead holds something that gets worse.
  • So just in time is a condition, not a policy. You cannot buffer finished work even if you want to. That is the one place where software is easier than manufacturing, and it is easy for a reason nobody would choose.

The second row deserves its own paragraph. Because finished work cannot be held, the only shock absorber this system has is the work-in-process queue in front of the constrained station. That is why the fourth rule is narrow rather than cautious: the queue is not one option among several, it is the only inventory the medium allows. A queue anywhere else is not a buffer, it is delay.

The consequence for learners

A factory amortises learning over repetition of the part: the thousandth bracket costs less than the first because the operator has made it a thousand times. Software never gets a thousandth bracket. All of its learning therefore has to be captured in the route — the checks, the documents, the templates, the setup reduction — because the route is the only thing that repeats.

That explains a fact which puzzles people moving from manufacturing into software: why software organisations put so much effort into process, tooling, documents and automation relative to the effort they put into any single change. It is not bureaucracy for its own sake. With no parts to repeat, the route is the only place a learning curve can live.

9. Mistake-proofing: prevent, detect at the act, limit the damage

Poka-yoke is Shigeo Shingo's word for a device that makes an error impossible, or shows it at the moment it happens. recalled Three levels, and they are not interchangeable:

  1. Prevent. The wrong action cannot be taken. A connector that only fits one way. A tracked field that is not reachable from outside the module that owns it, so no component can assign to it. A protected branch that refuses a direct push.
  2. Detect at the act. The error is caught where it is made, before anything downstream sees it. The compiler rejects the call; the check rejects the commit. The cost of detection is the cost of the act, not the cost of the journey.
  3. Limit the damage. The error happens and its consequences are bounded: the ref that keeps a dropped commit reachable, the archived transcript after its worktree is deleted, the baseline that stops a known failure from masking a new one.

Control versus warning

A device that refuses is a control. A device that alerts and continues is a warning. Both are legitimate; they are not the same strength. git commit --no-verify turns every hook into a warning. A branch ruleset that refuses direct pushes is a control.

A device fails silently

A broken mistake-proofing device is worse than an absent one. Absent means people look; broken means nobody does, because the device's whole purpose is to remove the need to look. This is why a new check is proven by removing the exact thing it must catch, once per shape it must catch, and why a list of tolerated failures has to be a written list rather than a count.

Where to spend it

Prevention costs design effort and removes flexibility, so it goes where failure is unrecoverable or invisible: work landing on the remote unverified, a reset discarding edits nobody committed, a delete that leaves nothing reachable, an identifier that silently lands in another component's range, a memory error that only appears under a different allocator. For a failure that is cheap to notice and cheap to fix, detection at the act is enough. For everything else, nothing — a device on a path nobody takes is overproduction.

10. Three practices that run the loop

These are Toyota's five Ss and three Ms, rewritten for a loop that builds software, plus the decision practice that sits upstream of both. They are described here by what they are for; the operational versions live with the project that uses them.

3M: judging a run

Reading half of the loop. Three axes, and they are not three names for the same problem:

  • muda — work that consumes effort and produces nothing. Eight categories: transport, inventory, motion, waiting, overproduction, over-processing, defects, unused talent. A category, not a size: one wasted call and forty are the same defect.
  • mura — unevenness between runs, which is what makes a single measurement meaningless and a comparison unfair.
  • muri — an unreasonable load on one actor, which produces muda elsewhere: a reviewer at the end of its context writes a shallow verdict; a worker with five items rushes the fifth.

The judgement is written as six lines before any decision is made:

run:      <ids>            commits: N   rounds: R
cost:     worker Xcalls/Ys  review Xcalls/Ys  wall A->B
muda:     <category>: <what, and what it cost>
mura:     <what varied against comparable runs>
muri:     <who was overburdened, with the number>
value:    <defects found, beads closed, gates now running>

Then decide, cheapest first: fix it now if it is one edit; gate it if a command can catch it; write it down if the fix is a rule; file it if none of those fit; drop it if the value was real. The value: line is what keeps the judgement honest, and it is deliberately not a metric.

Two traps when comparing runs, and they are the vocabulary from part 6 doing real work. Regression to the mean: an extreme run is followed by a less extreme one with no change at all, so "it improved after our fix" is the weakest claim available. Tampering: reacting to variation the process always has, which increases it. Both are why a finding needs a witness — a second instance, or a check that fails — and not a before-and-after of one run.

5S: filing what got in the way

Writing half of the loop. When a command, a gate or a documented step does not work first time, that is a defect in the environment, not in the person who hit it: every session that rediscovers it pays again. The report is three fields and one tracked item, then back to work.

And the closing rule is where part 9 enters: the fix is not complete until the strongest device that fits is in place — make the wrong action impossible, then fail at the moment it is taken, then write the instruction down.

The decision practice: what to optimise, and what to refuse

Upstream of both. For any proposed change — a new gate, a metric, a parallel-dispatch policy, a batch of items in one worker, a technique borrowed from a factory — ask three questions in order and stop at the first that fails:

  1. What is the reasoning, and what environment did the tool assume?
  2. Does that environment hold here? Check against the four rules.
  3. What replaces it, what is the witness, and which strength of device is it?

11. A playbook for your own software loop

Ten steps, in the order they pay. Each one has an output you can look at, so progress is visible rather than felt.

  1. Write the route. List the stations every change passes through, in order. If different kinds of change take different routes, you have no standard work yet, and everything below will drift. Output: one page, with a station list.
  2. Answer the environment question. Is any station dedicated to one kind of work? If not, you are in Ohno's setting and the pull-based mechanisms apply. If most stations are dedicated and volume is high, you are Ford's, and none of this transfers cleanly.
  3. Find your unit. Ask whether two units are exchangeable. If a documentation fix and a concurrency fix are both "one item", stop counting items, and look for a measure that survives the difference — lead time usually does.
  4. Time the route before changing it. Per item: when it became available, when work started, the time in each station, when it was verified on the shared branch. Without the first and last of those you cannot see the queue, and without the queue you cannot find the constraint. Output: a lead-time number per item and a queue time per item.
  5. State the pull, with a number. Choose how much work may be in flight, write the number down, and make the mechanism refuse beyond it. A limit that is silently exceeded is not a limit.
  6. Look for the constraint by lowering slack, one knob at a time. Raise parallelism until a station degrades; shorten a timeout until it fires; remove one item from the tolerated-failure list. Lower one, record the result, fix what shows, repeat. This is what the water-and-rocks image means in practice, and the discipline of one at a time is the condition for a stable control loop.
  7. Buy exactly one queue, in front of whichever station is slowest, and file every other wait as a defect with its duration.
  8. Make the queue visible, and act on it. A view nobody acts on is a dashboard. The test: does someone prepare for an item before it arrives, and does a pull decision change because the queue was visible? If neither, delete the view.
  9. Audit your devices. For each check you rely on, name its level: impossible, fails at the act, or an instruction. Then for each device, prove it by removing what it must catch. Anything that turns out to be an instruction is either upgraded or written down as a known risk.
  10. Run the judgement loop after every cycle. muda, mura, muri, value, written as six lines, then a decision per finding: fix, gate, document, file, or drop. This is the PDCA half, and it is what makes the other nine steps improve instead of calcifying.

The order matters, and the reason is arithmetic

Steps 4 and 5 come before step 6 for the same reason a measurement comes before a target: Little's Law and the utilisation curve are the only things that will tell you whether your pull limit is a policy or a wish. A team that sets a pull limit without timing the route has invented a number, and will discover whether it was right by argument.

12. Three corrections this work made to itself

Kept because they are more instructive than the conclusions. Each is a real error made and corrected while producing this material, and each illustrates one of the method's steps failing and then working.

One: reading the environment from the wrong feature

The first reading said a software loop is a project environment, because every work item is unique and has never been done before. That is wrong, and the reason is precise: the stability that matters is not stability of the work item but stability of the route. Toyota needed the route to hold still when the product changed; a loop whose stations are content-agnostic satisfies that even though every item is new. The lesson: when you check an application's assumptions, check the ones the mechanism actually depends on, not the ones that sound related.

Two: quoting the wrong system's rules

The three rules first recalled for Kanban — limit work in progress, visualise the work, and manage flow — belong to the modern Kanban method, a change-management approach for knowledge work. Toyota's rules are six and they are about the card. The two lists are not variants of each other; one is a mechanism and the other is a method named after it. The lesson: the concept-versus-application distinction applies to vocabulary too, and a term borrowed from a different environment imports that environment's assumptions silently.

Three: shorthand that assumed a shared reading

Early drafts said things like "we are in Ohno's environment, not Ford's", which is clear to anyone who has read the article and opaque to anyone who has not. The fix was not to remove the names but to explain each one where it first appears, and to attach an example from a real run. The lesson: a document for teaching has to be self-contained, and the failure mode is not jargon but unstated context. The same rule applies to a borrowed term: state its claim beside it, or do not use it.

14. What is new here, and what is not

A guide that calls itself revolutionary teaches students to skip the measurement. So this part is the honest accounting, because a reader is going to ask what is actually new and a teacher needs an answer that survives the question.

Nothing in parts 1 to 9 is new

Ford's moving line is 1913. Shewhart's split between common and special cause is 1931. Ohno's system was built between 1945 and the 1970s, and the card went plant-wide in 1963. Shingo's work on changeover and mistake-proofing is from the 1950s to the 1980s. Little's Law and Kingman's approximation are both 1961. Deming's funnel and the plan-do-check-act cycle are 1986. Goldratt's article is 2006, and the modern Kanban method is 2010. The three Ms and the five Ss are Toyota's, and decades old. Every term in the glossary is borrowed, and the glossary says so.

Five things that are new, or at least not usual

  1. The environment question is a step, not a caveat. Most writing that brings factory ideas to software imports both halves of Toyota: the reasoning and the specific tools, with a note that local conditions vary. Here one question is asked before anything else — is any station dedicated to a kind of work? — and the answer decides which half is legal. It takes a meeting to ask and it changes what you build.
  2. The parts-and-route split, with three testable consequences. Software has no standardised parts, only a standardised process. From that one fact: the buffer moves from the part to the station, levelling moves from the product mix to the arrival of work at the constrained station, and the learning curve moves from the part to the route. Each of the three is a claim that can be checked against a real loop, which is more than can be said for most transfer writing.
  3. Four rules with a refusal list attached. Not a list of rules that worked elsewhere, but four rules, each with what it forbids, plus twelve named refusals and the assumption each one carries. The refusals are the part a reader can move to their own environment without copying this project's answers, because they model the method rather than the result.
  4. Three practices built to run unattended. A judgement that is six lines and a five-way decision; a filing scheme of five buckets with a closing rule about device strength; a decision procedure of three questions. They are shaped so that a program can run them at three in the morning with no meeting and produce something a person can read. That design constraint does not exist in any of the source material, and it is why the practices look shorter and more mechanical than their ancestors.
  5. Auditing your own checks for strength. Classifying every check as prevent, detect at the act, limit the damage, or merely an instruction, and then finding that the project's own central rule about tracked state was an instruction. Neither the manufacturing literature nor the software literature routinely audits its own guarantees this way: they audit the process, not the devices that are supposed to protect it.

What would show this is wrong

A method that cannot fail is a belief. Three observations would falsify the parts that matter:

  • If lead time does not fall when work in process falls. Little's Law says it must. A failure means either the measurement is wrong or the loop is not a stable system, and both are worth knowing.
  • If the constraint turns out to be something the four rules cannot touch — a review whose capacity cannot be raised for policy reasons, say. Then the answer is a decision rather than more flow, and the model needs a fifth rule instead of a fifth knob.
  • If a substitution cannot be measured at all, such as the buffer per station. Then it is vocabulary rather than engineering, and it should be demoted to a hypothesis and labelled as one.

The failure this literature keeps having

Part 1 records it: Toyota published everything, invited competitors in, and fewer than 20% of Japanese manufacturers managed to implement any of it. Goldratt's article is a study of companies that adopted the vocabulary and not the measurement, and were worse off for it.

The same failure is available here. A team that adopts four rule names, puts up a board, sets a work-in-process limit, and never measures the lead time of a single item has done exactly what Hitachi did before it went back to its old ways. The test of whether this material helped is not whether people use the words. It is whether someone can say, for one item, when it became available, when it landed, and where the queue was.

15. Six objections, and honest answers

These are the ones a room asks. Each answer is short on purpose: an objection answered in a paragraph is an objection taken seriously.

“Our work is creative, so flow does not apply.”

The creativity is in the content of a change, and the content is never the same twice — that is exactly the point of part 8. The route repeats even though the content does not, and the route is where the queues, the waiting and the rework live. Nobody is proposing to standardise the thinking. The proposal is to standardise the sequence of stations around it, so that the thinking is not delayed by a queue.

“We cannot measure lead time; our items vary too much.”

Variation between items makes a comparison between two items unfair. It does not stop you answering “when did this become available” and “when did it land” for any item, which is all a lead time needs. Little's Law requires a stable system, not uniform items. If the average is meaningless because the spread is enormous, that is not a reason to stop measuring; it is the first finding, and the spread between two items is itself a muda question.

“WIP limits made people miserable — they sat idle.”

Idle is the intended behaviour when the downstream station is the constraint, and the misery usually comes from a limit that was set without saying what it protects. A limit with a number and a named reason reads as engineering; a limit with neither reads as an arbitrary rule from above, and gets gamed. That is the whole content of rule 3: choose the pull, and name what set it.

“We already do Scrum, or Kanban, or SAFe.”

Then check one thing: can you state the lead time of the last five items from available to landed, and where the queue was? If yes, most of this guide is confirmation and the refusal list is the part worth reading. If no, the vocabulary arrived and the measurement did not, which is a different project from the one this guide describes.

“AI agents change all of this.”

An agent removes the typing, not the queue. The work still has to be read by someone who did not write it, checked by something that can fail, and landed on a shared branch. What agents change is the cost of starting work, and that makes the pull limit more important rather than less, because idle capacity stops being the visible cost and becomes an invisible one. The constraint moves to review capacity, model rate, and human decisions — all of them queues, and all of them subject to part 5.measured This project runs that way, and the constraint it found first was the review, not the machine.

“This is just the theory of constraints with new labels.”

Partly, and the ancestry is stated in part 1 rather than hidden. The four concepts are Ford's and Ohno's, the constraint language is Goldratt's, and the queue arithmetic is standard. What is not in that tradition: the parts-and-route split applied to software, the refusal list, the practice artefacts, and the audit of device strength. Naming your ancestors is not a weakness in a method; claiming to have invented them is.

What not to do

Do not teach the vocabulary first. The words are labels for things the room can find in fifteen minutes: a queue, an idle station, a check that never fires, a wait nobody chose. Teach the question, let them find the thing, and then give the word. A room taught the words first will use them and measure nothing, which is the failure part 14 describes.

17. Running this Socratically, and what a course would need

The honest answer: this book does not do that

The guide explains. It states a rule, gives its reason, then gives an example. The Socratic order is the reverse: an example, the reader's prediction about it, the prediction failing, and only then the rule — which the reader states first and the book confirms second. Goldratt's own teaching device, the mentor in The Goal who answers every question with a question, is a harder version still: he refuses to give the insight at all.

Three parts lean in that direction without getting there. Part 12 states three beliefs that turned out wrong. Part 15 lists the objections and answers them. Part 14 says what would falsify the whole thing. Those are the only places where a reader is invited to hold a belief and then lose it. Everywhere else the answer comes first, which is the expository form and is appropriate for a reference, and is not teaching by discovery.

What the form requires, adapted for a recording

A live Socratic dialogue has an advantage a video does not: the mentor can ask about the student's own plant, and the student cannot escape into generalities. Three rules recover most of that:

  1. The viewer supplies the data. In the first minute the viewer is asked to pick one real item from their own work and to keep it. Every later question is asked about that item. A viewer who has not committed to one item cannot answer any of the questions, and the video stops working.
  2. Commit before the reveal. The wrong answer is stated fairly, then the video stops and asks for the viewer's own answer — out loud or on paper — before the next segment plays. A question the narrator answers three seconds later teaches that the answer is the narrator's, not the viewer's.
  3. The viewer states the rule last, in their own words. Each episode ends with the viewer writing one sentence and only then seeing the book's version. The two are allowed to differ; what matters is that the sentence came from an observation rather than from a slide.

One worked scene, from this material

This is the central move of part 3, written as a dialogue. It is seven beats, and the beats are labelled because the labels are the reusable part: the same sequence works for any technique a team is about to import.

A team lead, mid-sentence, on the subject of factory ideas.

student: Our work is different from a factory. Every task we do is unique — nobody has ever built this exact thing before. You cannot standardise a one-off.

jonah: How many pieces of work did your team finish last month?

student: About twelve.

Beat 2 — ask for the student's own data

jonah: Of those twelve, how many were written, then reviewed, then tested, then merged?

student: All twelve. Those are our steps.

jonah: Twelve different things. One route.

Beat 3 — repeat the student's words back

student: But the work is different every time. That is the point.

jonah: You said the work is different. Did you say the route was different?

student: …no. The work.

Beat 4 — force specificity

jonah: What was different about the fourth one and the seventh?

student: Size. One was a config change, one was a concurrency bug. Different files, different risks.

jonah: Who reviewed the seventh?

student: Sanne.

jonah: Did she review it differently from the fourth?

student: No. Same checklist, same commands.

jonah: So what repeated?

student: Fine. The route repeated.

Beat 5 — the counterexample, aimed at the student's new rule

student: So we standardise the route. We already have a document that says what the steps are.

jonah: A factory with a written route and no measurement produces what?

student: Product. Obviously.

jonah: It produces product and cannot tell you the lead time. Can you tell me yours?

student: For which item?

jonah: The seventh.

student: …not without going and looking.

Beat 6 — the distinction the student missed

jonah: Then which one is the standard work — the document, or the number?

student: The number is what the document is for.

jonah: Say it as a rule.

student: Standard work is a route you can time.

Beat 7 — hand it back to the student's own case

jonah: Write that down. Now go and find out when the seventh became available, and when it landed.

Why this sequence works, and what each beat is for: beat 2 gets data the student cannot argue with, because they produced it. Beat 3 makes the student correct their own sentence rather than defend against yours. Beat 4 removes the escape into generalities. Beat 5 tests the new rule and finds it incomplete, which is the step most teaching skips — the first correction is usually not the last. Beat 6 makes the student name the distinction. Beat 7 sends them to measure, so the insight arrives with a number attached to their own work.

Two rules for writing these, learned from the same material. The student's wrong belief must be defensible, stated as the student would state it, and never as a straw man; if the reader does not recognise themselves in it, the beat does nothing. And the mentor never supplies the missing word — the student says “the route repeated” and “a route you can time”, and those two phrases are the whole lesson.

19. Glossary

The terms, alphabetically, each as the claim it makes.

admission control
The decision at the door whether to accept new work. A pull limit is admission control for a delivery loop.
backpressure
The downstream tells the upstream to stop producing. The release rule in a pull system is one.
common cause / special cause
Shewhart's split: variation the process always has, versus variation from an event with an identifiable cause.
control device
A device that refuses the action, so the error cannot continue.
cost of change
What the next modification to this code will cost. The quantity lead time stands in for when a loop cannot price its items.
deadband
A band inside which a signal is ignored and no action is taken. Toyota's buffer rule — reduce below 5% red orders, increase above 10%, do nothing between — is one.
Goodhart's law
A measure adopted as a target stops measuring what it was chosen to measure, because the cheapest way to move the number becomes the work.
headroom
Capacity held in reserve. Near full utilisation it is worth several times its arithmetic share.
heijunka
Levelling production against a demand pattern. Refused here, because the demand is chosen rather than received.
hysteresis
The state depends on the direction of past changes. It is what a deadband produces, and it is the property that stops noise from driving decisions.
internal vs external setup
Setup that stops the machine versus setup performed while it runs. Moving work from the first to the second shortens the route without adding capacity.
just in time
Make what the next step needs, when it needs it, in the amount it needs. It requires a pull signal, short changeover, no defective parts, and levelled demand. The principle transfers to software; the per-part buffer does not, because no part recurs.
latency / throughput
Time per item, and items per unit time. Different numbers that trade against each other; the four rules optimise latency first.
Little's Law
Work in process = throughput × lead time, for any stable system.
muda / mura / muri
Work that produces nothing / unevenness between runs / unreasonable load on one actor.
one knob at a time
With a delay between a change and its effect, more than one change per cycle gives no attribution and can oscillate.
overfitting
Fitting a rule to the data you saw instead of the process that produced it.
PDCA
Plan, do, check, act: the standard improvement cycle, with the retrospective in the check and act seats.
poka-yoke
A device that makes an error impossible, or shows it where it happens. Three levels: prevent, detect at the act, limit the damage.
pull
The next station takes what it needs when it needs it, rather than the previous station pushing work forward. A pull needs a limit to be a limit.
regression to the mean
An extreme result is followed by a less extreme one with no change at all.
rolled throughput yield
The fraction of items passing every station without rework. Its inverse is the review-round count.
standard work
The written current best sequence, which improvement is measured against.
takt time
The pace a customer's demand sets. Refused here: no customer sets one.
tampering
Adjusting a stable process in response to common-cause variation, which increases the variation instead of reducing it.
utilisation cliff
Queueing delay grows roughly as ρ/(1−ρ), so delay explodes near full load.
warning device
A device that alerts and lets the action continue, so it depends on someone acting on the alert.
work-in-process limit
A cap on items started and not finished. In a factory it is a count of cards; here it is a number the loop refuses to exceed.
